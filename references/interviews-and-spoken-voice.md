# Interviews and spoken voice

Use this reference for interviews, Q&A responses, transcripts, quotations, panels, podcasts, and prose meant to sound spoken.

## Keep the person on the page

An interview answer records someone thinking in public. Improve its legibility while preserving the route the thought takes. A perfectly ordered answer often stops sounding spoken.

Default to a clean interview edit. Produce a polished statement only when the user requests one or the source is already written in that register.

## Preserve useful irregularity

Keep source-supported features that carry the speaker's mind:

- first-person pressure such as "I think," "for me," or "what worries me" when it locates the judgment;
- a repeated word that reveals the speaker's real emphasis;
- a qualification added after the main claim;
- an answer that reaches its point gradually;
- unequal answer and paragraph lengths;
- ordinary connective language;
- a thought that ends with a consequence, question, or limit still open.

Remove repetition that merely restarts the same sentence. Keep repetition that shows what the speaker returns to.

## Edit selectively

- Repair grammar when it distracts from meaning.
- Remove filler that delays the first useful thought.
- Cut duplicate explanations after the point is already clear.
- Preserve substantive hedges and uncertainty.
- Keep the speaker's concrete verbs and nouns when an abstract editorial term would sound more impressive.
- Let the question carry context; avoid rebuilding that context in the first sentence of every answer.
- Break a long answer where the speaker changes direction, not at uniform visual intervals.

## Avoid the polished-interview template

Watch for an editor imposing the same shape on every answer:

1. thesis sentence;
2. two explanatory paragraphs;
3. compact concluding line.

Also remove these signs of over-editing:

- every answer begins with a complete conceptual distinction;
- every paragraph advances through frictionless transitions;
- every answer ends with a verdict or quotable punchline;
- plain speech becomes a chain of abstract nouns;
- the speaker's first person disappears from judgments that are genuinely theirs;
- all answers share the same confidence, pace, length, and syntax;
- ambiguity is resolved beyond what the source supports;
- local repetitions are replaced by conspicuous synonym cycling.

One answer may be blunt. Another may circle the issue before arriving. A third may stop after the useful detail. Preserve that variation.

## Keep roughness authentic

Do not add "um," "you know," false starts, typos, slang, self-corrections, personal memories, or uncertainty that the source did not contain. Synthetic messiness is another performance.

For written interview responses, aim for credible correspondence: a knowledgeable person answering a question in their own words. Do not pretend the response was transcribed from spontaneous speech.

## Set the polish ceiling

Before returning the edit, ask:

- Would this person plausibly say the sentence aloud?
- Did the edit upgrade plain language into editorial language?
- Does every answer sound as though the same editor wrote it from scratch?
- Did each answer acquire a thesis and conclusion it did not need?
- Did clarity improve while the speaker's emphasis and uncertainty survive?
- Are some sentences allowed to be merely useful instead of memorable?

If the result feels too perfect, restore the strongest source phrasing and remove one layer of editorial explanation. Do not damage grammar or invent quirks.

## Example

Over-polished:

> At scale, execution integrity becomes the decisive test. The quote shown to a risk desk must survive intact through settlement.

Closer to a spoken expert:

> What worries me at scale is the gap between the quote a risk desk sees and the trade it actually gets. That gap gets expensive very quickly.

The second version locates the judgment in the speaker and follows the concern they expressed. It remains clean without sounding engineered as a thesis.
