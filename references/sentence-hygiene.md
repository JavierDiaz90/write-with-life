# Sentence hygiene

Use this audit after the structure, emphasis, and narrator are working. Treat every item as a diagnostic clue, not a ban.

## Delete what does no work

Remove or replace:

- chatbot greetings, praise, offers, and sign-offs embedded in standalone prose;
- announcements of the next point instead of the point itself;
- headings restated by the sentence beneath them;
- generic introductions, summaries, and optimistic endings;
- inflated claims of importance, legacy, transformation, or inevitability unsupported by evidence;
- vague appeals to experts, critics, reports, or conventional wisdom;
- fake objections and alternatives introduced only to be dismissed;
- decorative formatting, bold labels, or lists that hide thin material;
- duplicate qualifications added to repair an overstatement;
- sentences that say only that something is interesting, important, complex, or worth noting.

Cut empty material instead of making it sound more conversational.

## Rebuild weak sentences

Watch for clusters of these habits:

- abstract nouns carrying actions that concrete subjects could perform;
- passive constructions that conceal an important actor;
- simple verbs replaced by inflated phrases;
- present participles attached to facts to simulate analysis;
- repeated subject-verb sentence openings with no deliberate rhythm;
- synonym cycling that renames the same person or thing;
- forced symmetry, triads, ranges, or punchlines;
- reversal templates that manufacture emphasis by rejecting one idea and replacing it with another: "not X but Y," "it isn't X; it's Y," "doesn't need X; needs Y," "less about X than Y," and similar constructions;
- stacked adjectives, intensifiers, and promotional language;
- strings of polished mid-length sentences with identical weight;
- answers or sections that all open with a thesis and close with a polished verdict;
- metaphors or sayings that sound memorable but make the claim less precise;
- transitions such as "additionally" or "moreover" where no relationship has been established;
- casual stage business such as "honestly" or "here's the thing" that performs intimacy without earning it.

Repair the thought, not only the watched word. Rewrite the paragraph when a local edit leaves the same mechanical pattern intact.

State the real claim directly instead of staging a correction. Mention the rejected idea separately only when the reader is genuinely likely to hold it and the distinction changes the argument. Treat reversal templates as prohibited by default, especially in openings, topic sentences, and conclusions.

## Preserve deliberate craft

Do not automatically remove:

- em dashes, en dashes, parentheses, semicolons, or curly quotation marks;
- one-word or fragmentary sentences;
- groups of three;
- repetition or parallel syntax;
- first person, contractions, slang, or technical vocabulary;
- long sentences or unusually short paragraphs;
- ambiguity, mixed feelings, self-correction, or an unresolved ending.

Judge the function. A dash can enact an interruption. A fragment can stop the reader. Repetition can build pressure. A triad can create completion. Keep the device when removing it weakens the intended effect.

Follow the target publication's typography and grammar conventions. Typography is not evidence of authorship.

## Protect factual integrity

- Name a source when the source material names one.
- Remove an unsupported generalization rather than inventing evidence for it.
- Preserve meaningful qualifiers; remove only redundant hedging.
- Do not turn correlation into causation or possibility into certainty.
- Do not add dates, locations, sensory details, reactions, quotations, rankings, or examples.
- Do not create first-person experience the user did not provide.
- Preserve precise technical terms when simplification would change the meaning.

## Run the final ear test

Ask:

1. Does any sentence sound as though it was selected because it is broadly acceptable rather than exactly right?
2. Do paragraph endings repeatedly deliver a slogan, fragment, or miniature revelation?
3. Are strong claims backed by stronger material than weak claims?
4. Does syntax follow the movement of thought?
5. Is a supposedly human detail actually supported?
6. Has cleanup erased useful strangeness, tension, or personality?
7. Did the rewrite create emphasis with a "not X; Y" reversal instead of earning it through evidence, position, or detail?
8. Did the cleanup remove so much local variation that one editorial voice now dominates the whole piece?
