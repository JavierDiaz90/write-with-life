# Structure and emphasis

Use this reference to rebuild a piece longer than a few paragraphs or any draft whose main problem is flow, hierarchy, pacing, or a predictable shape.

## Contents

## Find the governing movement

Structure is a relationship among selected pieces of material, not a template placed on top of them. Work only with what the source supports.

Sort the material by function:

- **Action or scene:** something happens in time.
- **Evidence:** a fact, example, quotation, or observation earns a claim.
- **Context:** the reader receives information needed to understand the next move.
- **Interpretation:** the writer explains what the material means.
- **Complication:** a fact, limit, or counterforce changes the reader's understanding.
- **Texture:** a concrete detail makes the subject present.

A piece made only of context feels like notes. A piece made only of interpretation feels unearned. A piece made only of scenes may feel vivid but meaningless.

## Select an architecture

Choose the simplest form that gives the material momentum.

| Architecture | Use it when | Governing movement |
|---|---|---|
| Straight chronology | Sequence and causality matter, especially in complex events | before -> disruption -> response -> consequence |
| Scene-context-scene | One or more supported moments embody the larger subject | lived moment -> meaning -> changed moment |
| Question-led investigation | The writer or reader genuinely needs to discover something | question -> attempts -> evidence -> answer or better question |
| Braided strands | Two or three lines illuminate one another and truly converge | A1 -> B1 -> A2 -> B2 -> convergence |
| Representative moments | A person, place, or institution is best understood through selected encounters | revealing moment -> context -> contrasting moment -> portrait |
| Argument with a turn | The apparent explanation is incomplete | common view -> evidence -> complication -> revised view |
| Journey through a system | Following a person, object, transaction, or decision exposes a larger mechanism | entry -> transformations -> consequences -> return to reader |
| Mosaic | No honest single chronology exists, but fragments accumulate toward one meaning | image or vignette sequence -> pattern -> resonance |

Do not braid unrelated topics merely for variety. Do not open in the middle of an event unless the later explanation repays the withheld context. Use chronology when clever rearrangement would make a difficult subject harder to understand.

## Make a beat plan

Plan five to nine beats for a substantial piece. For each beat, state:

1. What new thing happens or becomes clear?
2. Why does it appear here?
3. What question, pressure, image, or consequence carries the reader forward?

If two adjacent beats perform the same job, merge them. If a beat has no answer to the second question, move or cut it.

### Common weak plan

1. Announce the topic.
2. List three benefits.
3. Explain each benefit at equal length.
4. Summarize the benefits.
5. End with generic optimism.

### Stronger product or idea plan

1. Put the reader inside the costly or frustrating status quo.
2. Reveal why the obvious solution has failed.
3. Introduce the new possibility as a change in what the reader can do.
4. Let capabilities appear through a coherent workflow or use case.
5. Acknowledge the real boundary or tradeoff.
6. Show the practical consequence.
7. End with the next meaningful action.

The stronger plan is not automatically a scene. It is a sequence of changing understanding.

## Control emphasis

Use several forms of emphasis, but do not stack all of them on every important point.

- **Position:** put important material at the opening, a turn, or the end of a sentence, paragraph, section, or piece.
- **Duration:** spend more words or time where the reader should remain.
- **Compression:** dispatch background quickly so it does not compete with the center.
- **Isolation:** let a consequential sentence or image stand without immediately explaining it away.
- **Specificity:** replace a general assertion with the strongest supported example.
- **Contrast:** place unlike facts, scenes, or sentence shapes next to each other.
- **Return:** repeat a key image or phrase only when its meaning has changed.
- **Omission:** trust the reader to cross small gaps; do not narrate every transition.

Paragraphs should not all have the same size or shape. Unequal material deserves unequal space.

## Build transitions from thought

Make each paragraph answer, extend, narrow, complicate, or overturn the previous one. Use generic connectors only when they name a real relationship.

Useful tests:

- What does the reader believe before this paragraph?
- What do they believe after it?
- Why could this paragraph not appear two pages earlier or later?

Headings should reveal genuine divisions in the piece. Do not use headings to conceal the absence of movement.

## Open honestly

Choose an opening that contains energy already present in the material:

- a consequential action;
- a specific observation;
- an unanswered question;
- a contradiction;
- a result whose cause the piece will uncover;
- a clear assertion with pressure behind it.

Establish the reader promise within the opening movement. Avoid atmospheric scenes unrelated to the central subject, fake suspense, dictionary definitions, broad historical panoramas, and announcements about what the piece will cover.

## End deliberately

An ending may:

- complete the action or answer the governing question;
- return to an opening image with altered meaning;
- show a concrete consequence;
- leave the central tension honestly unresolved;
- narrow from the system back to a person or decision;
- open onto the next necessary action.

Do not summarize the entire piece again. Do not manufacture uplift, certainty, symmetry, or a moral. The ending must deliver, complicate, or consciously refuse the promise made at the beginning.

## Craft lineage

These principles synthesize craft practices described by working writers and reporters, especially:

- John McPhee, ["Structure"](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2013/01/14/structure): let the factual material constrain the form; use arrangement to create attraction.
- Jon Franklin, [Nieman Storyboard interview](https://niemanstoryboard.org/2004/11/18/an-interview-with-jon-franklin/): find one story, report actions and meaning, and outline before drafting.
- Kim Cross and other narrative reporters, [Nieman's scene-reporting collection](https://niemanstoryboard.org/2025/10/03/journalists-on-reporting-and-writing-scenes/): gather concrete detail and control the movement between scene and summary.
- Lauren Kessler, ["Endings"](https://niemanstoryboard.org/2023/05/19/narrative-writing-story-structure-endings/): design the ending as part of the whole and allow honest ambiguity.
- Roy Peter Clark, ["If I Were a Carpenter"](https://www.poynter.org/archive/2002/if-i-were-a-carpenter-the-tools-of-the-writer/): distribute strong material, control pace, and use placement for emphasis.
